Tides arise from the gravitational pull of the moon and, to a lesser degree, the sun. As the earth rotates, the ocean bulges both toward and away from the moon, producing two high tides and two low tides at most coastal locations each day. The exact timing shifts by roughly fifty minutes daily, because the moon advances along its orbit while the earth turns beneath it. Coastal geometry modifies the pattern considerably: narrow inlets and shallow shelves can amplify the range from under a metre to more than ten. A few locations have only one high tide per day, owing to the way these local effects combine.
